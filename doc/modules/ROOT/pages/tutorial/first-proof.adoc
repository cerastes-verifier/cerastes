= A first proof

This chapter verifies three small functions. Along the way it introduces the
three annotations you will use most: a postcondition (`CERASTES_ENSURES`), a
precondition (`CERASTES_REQUIRES`) and a check in the middle of a function
(`CERASTES_CHECK`).

== A postcondition

Here is a function that returns the larger of two numbers, with a contract that
says what "larger" means:

[source,c]
----
include::example$tutorial/first_proof_max.c[tag=source]
----

`CERASTES_ENSURES(r, ...)` is a *postcondition*: a promise about the moment the
function returns. Its first argument, `r`, names the returned value, and the
rest is an ordinary C expression over `r` and the parameters. This one says that
the result is at least `a`, at least `b`, and one of the two. A function that
returned `a + b`, or `INT_MAX`, would meet the first half and not the second.

The contract goes between the parameter list and the body. `<cerastes.h>`
defines the macro; an ordinary compiler sees nothing there.

Verify it, in the examples directory
(xref:tutorial/index.adoc#_before_you_start[Before you start]):

[source,sh]
----
../../../../../build/cerastes verify first_proof_max.c
----

[source,console]
----
first_proof_max.c: verified (1 function; one translation unit: assumes that functions defined elsewhere satisfy the contracts declared here)
----

cerastes considered every pair of `int` values at once, followed both branches,
and showed that each `return` meets the postcondition. The rest of the line is a
reminder of what the result rests on; xref:tutorial/calls.adoc[Calls and
contracts] explains it.

== When the proof fails

Now make a mistake: the last `return` gives `a` instead of `b`.

[source,c]
----
include::example$tutorial/first_proof_max_wrong_branch_error.c[tag=source]
----

[source,console]
----
first_proof_max_wrong_branch_error.c:14: error: postcondition: the postcondition of `max_of` may not hold
first_proof_max_wrong_branch_error.c:9: note: this is the postcondition
----

An error has three parts. The *line* is where the problem happens: here the
`return` whose value breaks the promise. The *category*, `postcondition`, says
what kind of thing could not be proved; every category is described on
xref:error-messages.adoc[Error messages]. The *note* points at the second place
that matters, the clause of the contract that does not hold.

cerastes does not tell you which inputs go wrong; you work that out from the
line and the clause. That `return` is reached when `a <= b`, and then `a` may be
smaller than `b`.

== A precondition

The absolute value of an `int` looks simpler still. Here is a first attempt,
with a contract that says the result is not negative and is `x` or `-x`:

[source,c]
----
include::example$tutorial/first_proof_abs_no_requires_error.c[tag=source]
----

[source,console]
----
first_proof_abs_no_requires_error.c:10: error: overflow: negating the smallest value of `int` overflows
first_proof_abs_no_requires_error.c:13: error: overflow: negating the smallest value of `int` overflows
----

The most negative `int`, `INT_MIN`, has no positive counterpart in two's
complement, so `-x` overflows for it, which is undefined behaviour in C.
cerastes checks every integer operation for overflow, whether or not a contract
mentions it (xref:tutorial/integers.adoc[the next chapter] is about this), and
it reports two: the `return -x` in the body, and the `-x` in the postcondition,
which is C arithmetic too and is checked for every `x`.

A function that cannot handle an input says so with a *precondition*:

[source,c]
----
include::example$tutorial/first_proof_abs.c[tag=source]
----

[source,console]
----
first_proof_abs.c: verified (2 functions; one translation unit: assumes that functions defined elsewhere satisfy the contracts declared here)
----

`CERASTES_REQUIRES(x != INT_MIN)` is a promise the *caller* makes. Inside
`abs_value`, and in its postcondition, cerastes may rely on it, so both
negations are safe. At every call, cerastes
checks that the promise is kept.

The caller, `distance_from_zero`, is verified too, and this shows how a caller
sees a function. cerastes does not look into the body of `abs_value`. It checks that
`-5` meets the precondition, then knows only what the postcondition says:
the result is not negative, and is `-5` or `5`. That is enough to prove that
it is `5`.

A caller that breaks the precondition is reported at the call:

[source,c]
----
include::example$tutorial/first_proof_abs_min_caller_error.c[tag=source]
----

[source,console]
----
first_proof_abs_min_caller_error.c:22: error: precondition: the precondition of `abs_value` may not hold
first_proof_abs_min_caller_error.c:10: note: this is the requirement of `abs_value`
----

The error is in `distance_from_zero`, not in `abs_value`: the caller broke its
side of the contract. The note shows the requirement it did not meet.

== Checking a fact on the way

`CERASTES_CHECK` states a fact at a point in the body. cerastes proves it there,
and from then on may use it. It is useful to find out what the verifier knows
when a proof fails, and to document a step of an argument:

[source,c]
----
include::example$tutorial/first_proof_check.c[tag=source]
----

[source,console]
----
first_proof_check.c: verified (1 function; one translation unit: assumes that functions defined elsewhere satisfy the contracts declared here)
----

A check that does not hold is reported at its line:

[source,c]
----
include::example$tutorial/first_proof_check_false_check_error.c[tag=source]
----

[source,console]
----
first_proof_check_false_check_error.c:14: error: check: this `check` may not hold
----

A check is never executed: like the other annotations it expands to nothing for
an ordinary compiler. The C `assert` macro is checked in the same way by
cerastes, and also runs in your build
(xref:verifying.adoc#assert[assert]).

== What you have learned

* A contract is written between the parameters and the body: `CERASTES_REQUIRES`
  for what the caller must make true, `CERASTES_ENSURES(r, ...)` for what the
  function makes true when it returns.
* cerastes proves the postcondition on every path, assuming the precondition;
  it proves the precondition at every call.
* Overflow is checked everywhere, without a contract asking for it.
* An error names a category and a line, and a note points at the clause
  involved.
